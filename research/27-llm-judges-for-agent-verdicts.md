# LLM Judges for Agent Verdicts: Failure Modes, Fixes, Abstention, and Validation

Scope: this report is about the four narrow places our evaluation harness uses an LLM judge, layered on top of a deterministic verifier that compares End state to a reference. It does not cover general chatbot response grading except where that literature is the only source of measured numbers. Every number below is attributed to a specific source (title, URL, date). Where a claim could not be verified against a primary source, it is marked "unverified" rather than presented as fact.

## 1. Known judge failure modes, with numbers

**Position bias.** Simply swapping which answer is shown first changes GPT-4's verdict in roughly a third of cases: with the default prompt GPT-4 gives a consistent verdict in only 65.0 percent of paired comparisons (66.2 percent with a "rename" prompt variant), and weaker judges are far less consistent (Claude-v1 23.8 percent to 56.2 percent, GPT-3.5 46.2 percent to 51.2 percent, depending on prompt). Source: "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena," Zheng et al., arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685, submitted June 9, 2023, NeurIPS 2023. A companion paper shows this can be exploited: reordering two answers let the weaker model, Vicuna-13B, "win" against ChatGPT on 66 of 80 test queries (82.5 percent) when ChatGPT was the judge. Source: "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators," Wang et al., arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926, May 29, 2023, ACL 2024. A 2026 systematic study found baseline position bias in modern frontier judges (Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash, Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4o, Llama 3.3-70B) is now much smaller, 0.04 or less, than the 2023-era papers reported, and that the classic fix (swap and average both orderings) is inconsistent on modern judges, helping some models (Gemini 2.5 Pro plus 4.6 points) and hurting others (GPT-4o minus 2.4 points), and actively hurting all models on adversarial data. Source: "Judging the Judges: A Systematic Evaluation of Bias Mitigation Strategies in LLM-as-a-Judge Pipelines," arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178, 2026. This is a direct contradiction of the widely cited 2023 recommendation and should be treated as the more current evidence.

**Verbosity bias.** Padding a correct answer with repeated or irrelevant content ("repetitive list" attack) fools GPT-3.5 and Claude-v1 judges 91.3 percent of the time, versus only 8.7 percent for GPT-4, which is far more resistant but not immune. Source: Zheng et al. 2023, as above.

**Self-preference / self-enhancement bias.** GPT-4 rates its own answers about 10 percentage points higher (win rate) than human evaluators do; Claude-v1 shows an even larger 25 point gap (Zheng et al. 2023, with the authors cautioning the sample size limits confidence). A dedicated study found GPT-4's out-of-the-box self-recognition accuracy is about 73.5 percent, GPT-3.5 about 53.5 percent (near chance), and that fine-tuning on just 500 examples pushes self-recognition above 90 percent; self-recognition capability correlates with self-preference (Kendall's tau rose from 0.41 to 0.74 for GPT-3.5 after fine-tuning), though the correlation did not hold as cleanly for every model tested (Llama 2 moved the opposite direction). Source: "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations," Panickssery, Bowman, Feng, arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076, April 19, 2024, NeurIPS 2024. A separate paper complicates the causal story: judges rate lower-perplexity, more familiar or fluent text more favorably regardless of whether it is self-generated, suggesting some "self-preference" is really a fluency artifact. Source: "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge," Wataoka, Takahashi, Ri, arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819, v2 June 21, 2025.

**Overall reliability ceiling and taxonomy.** GPT-4 as judge reaches roughly 80 to 85 percent agreement with humans on MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, close to the 82 percent human-human agreement ceiling on MT-Bench (Zheng et al. 2023). A 2024 to 2025 survey organizes biases into task-agnostic (position, length or verbosity, concreteness, recency) and judgment-specific (self-enhancement, authority bias, sycophancy) families, but does not offer a single consolidated numeric table; its contribution is taxonomy, not new measurement. Source: "A Survey on LLM-as-a-Judge," Gu et al., arxiv.org/html/2411.15594v6, November 2024, latest revision October 19, 2025.

**G-Eval.** Using GPT-4 with chain-of-thought and form-filling, correlation with human judgment on SummEval is moderate at best: Spearman correlation of 0.582 for coherence, 0.507 for consistency, 0.455 for fluency, 0.547 for relevance, each a step up from a GPT-3.5 backbone (0.440, 0.386, 0.424, 0.385 respectively) but still far from perfect. G-Eval-4 also shows a length/generation-source bias: it "always gives higher scores to GPT-3.5-generated summaries than to human-written summaries, even when human judges prefer the human-written summaries." Source: "G-Eval: NLG Evaluation using GPT-4 with Better Human Alignment," Liu et al., arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634, March 29, 2023, EMNLP 2023.

**Prometheus 2 and fine-tuned judges generally.** Prometheus 2's own paper reports Pearson correlation up to 0.898 with Claude-3-Opus as reference and 0.555 with humans on FLASK, and pairwise ranking accuracy from 71.96 percent (MT-Bench Human Judgment) to 90.65 percent (Preference-Bench). Source: Kim et al., arxiv.org/abs/2405.01535, May 2, 2024, EMNLP 2024. An independent study, however, finds fine-tuned judges (JudgeLM, PandaLM, Auto-J, Prometheus) collapse well below GPT-4 out of domain and especially under adversarial framing: on the adversarial LLMBar set, JudgeLM scores 23.1 percent, PandaLM 16.5 percent, Auto-J 20.9 percent, versus GPT-4-1106 at 64.2 percent. Source: "An Empirical Study of LLM-as-a-Judge for LLM Evaluation: Fine-tuned Judge Model is not a General Substitute for GPT-4," arxiv.org/abs/2403.02839, Findings of ACL 2025. These two sources disagree in degree: self-reported fine-tuned-judge papers look systematically more favorable than independent replications, most likely because their own benchmarks are closer to the fine-tuning distribution.

**JudgeBench and RewardBench 2.** On JudgeBench, GPT-4o with a vanilla prompt scores only 50.86 percent overall against a 50 percent random baseline; even the best-prompted GPT-4o reaches 56.57 percent, and Gemini-1.5-pro scores 47.14 percent, below chance. The best judge tested, o3-mini at high reasoning effort, reaches 80.86 percent, and fine-tuned judges mostly underperform prompted frontier models (Prometheus2-7b, 34.86 percent). Source: "JudgeBench," Tan et al., arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784, October 16, 2024, ICLR 2025. RewardBench 2 found leading reward models score about 20 points lower on average than on the original RewardBench, meaning the original benchmark overestimated reliability, and that downstream RLHF performance saturates across reward models scoring 49.8 to 68.5 on the new benchmark, so score differences in that range do not reliably predict which reward model is best. Source: "RewardBench 2," Malik et al., arxiv.org/abs/2506.01937, June 2, 2025.

**Judges evaluating agent trajectories (the closest analogue to our End state judging).** This is the most directly relevant literature and the numbers are worse than for chat grading. On TRAIL, a benchmark for localizing and categorizing agent trace errors, even the best model (Gemini 2.5 Pro) reaches only 18.3 percent joint accuracy on GAIA traces and 5.0 percent on SWE-Bench traces, and nearly all models score F1 of 0.00 on "context handling failures" as an error category, a near-total blind spot. Source: "TRAIL: Trace Reasoning and Agentic Issue Localization," Deshpande et al., arxiv.org/abs/2505.08638, May 13, 2025. On AgentRewardBench, 1,302 human-reviewed web-agent trajectories with human inter-annotator agreement of 89.3 percent, the best LLM judge (GPT-4o) reaches only 69.8 percent precision, no judge exceeds 70 percent precision, and judges systematically overestimate agent success; a rule-based evaluator errs the other way, at only 55.9 percent recall on WebArena. Source: "AgentRewardBench," Lu et al., arxiv.org/abs/2504.08942, April 11, 2025, v2 October 7, 2025. A 2026 paper on false-success detection found 45 to 48 percent of failures in single-control tau2-bench domains get mislabeled as successes by LLM judges, rising to 75.8 percent among AppWorld coding-agent trajectories with confident self-reported status claims, and that across five judge models and five prompt strategies no configuration exceeds AUROC 0.65 at telling true from false success; trajectories with confident closing language score 0.27 to 0.36 points more "completed" on average regardless of actual outcome, direct evidence of sycophancy to the transcript. Source: "From Confident Closing to Silent Failure: Characterizing False Success in LLM Agents," Advani, arxiv.org/abs/2606.09863, June 1, 2026.

**Cross-benchmark validity audit (the source of the "18.5 percent" figure).** A 2026 audit manually re-labeled 496 tasks across four tool-calling benchmark families and found the official labels disagree with expert judgment in 92 cases, an aggregate misalignment rate of 18.5 percent, broken down as tau2-Bench Retail 9.8 percent, BFCL v4 20.0 percent, MCP-Atlas 13.5 percent, and LiveMCPBench 30.5 percent. This is an aggregate misalignment rate across benchmark labeling and grading pipelines, not a number specific to tau2's natural-language assertions alone, though the paper does document a concrete tau2 nl_assertion failure: a task marked failed solely because a response lacked the exact substring "1628," despite correct tool execution and correct end state. The same paper found LiveMCPBench reproducibility spread by 18.9 percentage points (57.9 to 76.8 percent) across 23 repeated runs of the identical benchmark, which is pure judge or environment noise. Source: "Benchmarking the Benchmarks: A Validity Audit of Tool-Calling Evaluation," Vaghasiya, Bhat, Mohsin, Aali, arxiv.org/html/2607.02577, June 30, 2026. We could not find any source that ties an 18.5 percent figure specifically to tau2 nl_assertions in isolation; treat the number as an aggregate validity-audit statistic, useful context but not a clean nl_assertions-only figure.

**The "~40 percent constraint misrepresentation" figure.** A 2026 paper on RL training environments for agents reports: "real-world analysis found constraint misrepresentation in approximately 40 percent of positively reinforced episodes and fabrication in approximately 3.8 percent of episodes under outcome-only rewards." AgenticAI-Supervisor is that paper's own name for its RL-gym and reward-shaping framework, not a separate widely known benchmark. Source: "Beyond Static Evaluation: Building Simulation Environments for Scalable Agentic RL," arxiv.org/html/2607.05773v1, July 7, 2026. One of our research passes independently searched for "AgenticAI-Supervisor" and could not find it, which is worth flagging: the name is real but obscure, is this paper's internal framework name rather than an established benchmark, and the 40 percent figure describes reward-signal reliability under outcome-only RL training, not literally a judge's misalignment rate on held-out End state comparisons. Use this number as directional evidence that agents get rewarded despite misrepresenting whether they honored constraints, not as a precise judge-accuracy statistic.

**Judge hacking.** Minimal, content-free responses can trigger false-positive rewards from generative judges. A bare colon or the phrase "Thought process:" elicited false-positive rates up to 35 percent and 28.9 percent respectively from GPT-4o judges; reasoning models were far more robust (GPT-o1 averaged about 0.9 percent, worst case 3.4 percent; Claude-4 worst case 4.1 to 11.3 percent), but open-weight and specialized reward models were far worse (Qwen2.5-72B up to 90.9 percent FPR on math, a "General-Verifier" reward model hit 66.8 percent from a single blank space). This affects leading proprietary systems, not only weak models. Source: "One Token to Fool LLM-as-a-Judge," Zhao et al., arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794, July 11, 2025.

## 2. Techniques that measurably improve judges

**Reference-guided grading and chain-of-thought.** Giving GPT-4 a reference solution before grading a math question cut its failure rate from 70 percent (14 of 20, default prompt) to 30 percent (chain-of-thought) to 15 percent (reference-guided grading). Source: Zheng et al. 2023 (MT-Bench), as above. Pairwise and pointwise (single-answer) grading performed similarly well against humans on MT-Bench (85 percent versus 84 percent) and Chatbot Arena (87 percent versus 85 percent), so the reference-guided and chain-of-thought effects matter more than the pairwise-versus-pointwise choice for tasks with a checkable ground truth, which is our case (a).

**Rubric or checklist decomposition into atomic yes/no questions.** Decomposing a holistic evaluation into a checklist of atomic Boolean questions, tested across 12 evaluator models, improves average cross-model agreement by 0.45 and improves Spearman and Kendall correlation with humans by about 0.10 on average, with inter-model Fleiss' kappa reaching up to 0.72 on some aspects. Source: "CheckEval: A reliable LLM-as-a-Judge framework for evaluating text generation using checklists," arxiv.org/abs/2403.18771, EMNLP 2025. The related "Rubrics as Rewards" method turns rubrics into checklist-style RL reward signals, and beats holistic Likert-based LLM-judge rewards by up to 31 percent on HealthBench and 7 percent on GPQA-Diamond, and reduces performance variance across judge model sizes. Source: arxiv.org/abs/2507.17746, July 2025, updated October 2025. We searched specifically for "RubricForge" as named in the task brief and could not find it as a published paper or tool; the closest match is "Generating and Refining Dynamic Evaluation Rubrics for LLM-as-a-Judge," arxiv.org/abs/2605.30568, 2026, which fine-tunes a dedicated rubric generator via preference learning against a stronger meta-judge, and reports the fine-tuned 14B generator outperforming Claude Sonnet 4 at rubric generation, with MT-Bench pairwise agreement rising from 81.72 to 83.69 percent after fine-tuning. Treat "RubricForge" as a name we could not verify.

**Multiple samples with majority vote.** Sampling a judge three times and taking the majority verdict raised balanced accuracy on SummaC from 69.8 to 72.3 percent for DeepSeek-R1 and 79.4 to 80.6 percent for Qwen-3, but intra-rater reliability (Krippendorff's alpha across three runs) is itself low and model-dependent (0.265 for Llama 3.1, 0.507 for DeepSeek-R1, 0.563 for Qwen-3), and forcing temperature to zero (removing sampling variance entirely) hurt accuracy for DeepSeek-R1, from 72.3 back to 69.3 percent. Source: "Rating Roulette: Self-Inconsistency in LLM-As-A-Judge Frameworks," Haldar and Hockenmaier, arxiv.org/abs/2510.27106, October 31, 2025.

**Jury of diverse models (PoLL).** A panel of three models from different families (Command-R 35B, Claude Haiku, GPT-3.5) beat a single large judge (GPT-4 Turbo) on agreement with humans: Cohen's kappa of 0.763 to 0.906 versus GPT-4's 0.627 to 0.841 on KILT datasets, and Pearson correlation of 0.917 versus 0.817 on Chatbot Arena Hard, at 7 to 8 times lower API cost, with smaller score variance (standard deviation 2.2 versus 6.1 for a single GPT-3.5 judge). Source: "Replacing Judges with Juries: Evaluating LLM Generations with a Panel of Diverse Models," Verga et al., arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796, April 2024.

**Giving the judge tools (agentic judges).** This is the single largest measured improvement we found. Agent-as-a-Judge, which lets a judge agent inspect intermediate artifacts and traces rather than just reading a transcript, raises alignment with human consensus from 60.38 to 90.44 percent (OpenHands), 65.30 to 83.88 percent (GPT-Pilot), and 84.15 to 88.52 percent (MetaGPT) compared to plain LLM-as-judge on the same systems, at 97.64 percent lower cost and 97.72 percent less time than full human evaluation. Source: "Agent-as-a-Judge: Evaluate Agents with Agents," Zhuge et al., arxiv.org/abs/2410.10934, October 2024, ICML 2025. A tool-integrated RL-trained judge (TIR-Judge) that can call a code executor surpasses strong non-tool reasoning judges by up to 6.4 percent pointwise and 7.7 percent pairwise across seven benchmarks. Source: arxiv.org/abs/2510.23038, 2025. A separately cited result: giving a judge a code-execution tool to verify a solution, rather than just narrate about it, raised agreement with ground truth from below 42 percent to about 72 percent, though we could not independently confirm the primary paper behind this citation beyond the survey that quotes it (arxiv.org/abs/2510.24367), so treat the exact figure as secondhand.

**Structured output and temperature.** A dedicated 480,000-inference study found temperature is the dominant lever for judge consistency: at temperature 0.01 consistency is essentially 1.00 and error rate 0.00 to 0.01 across models, while at temperature 3.0 consistency collapses (to 0.57 for one model) and error rate spikes to 0.49; temperature's effect on raw agreement accuracy is smaller than its effect on consistency. Source: "The Necessity of Setting Temperature in LLM-as-a-Judge," arxiv.org/abs/2603.28304, 2026.

**Fine-tuned versus prompted frontier judges.** See section 1: fine-tuned judges (JudgeLM, PandaLM, Auto-J, Prometheus) beat GPT-4 in-domain but generalize and resist adversarial framing far worse; the disagreement between Prometheus's own paper and the independent replication (arxiv.org/abs/2403.02839) is real and should be treated as a live disagreement in the literature rather than resolved.

**Evidence citation and hiding irrelevant context.** These are the weakest-evidenced techniques in the brief. We could not find a primary paper isolating "require the judge to quote the exact span" as a standalone, benchmarked intervention with its own hallucination-reduction number; the closest evidence is the tool-verification result above, which is about running code, not quoting text. Similarly, "hide irrelevant context from the judge" is well established as practitioner folk wisdom (masking distractors, evaluating without conversation history, preferring binary rubrics over fine-grained numeric scales) but we could not find a controlled academic ablation quantifying its effect. Both should be treated as good practice supported by adjacent evidence and design logic, not as separately measured wins.

## 3. Abstention specifically

The strongest, most directly on-topic source is "Trust or Escalate: LLM Judges with Provable Guarantees for Human Agreement," Jung, Brahman, Choi, arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370, July 25, 2024, ICLR 2025. Their method samples the judge under several simulated annotator personas, uses agreement across personas as a confidence signal, and escalates low-confidence cases to a stronger judge rather than guessing. Against a target of 85 percent human agreement on a Chatbot Arena subset, plain GPT-4 with full coverage only reaches 77.8 percent agreement and never meets the target (0 percent guarantee success rate), while their cascaded, confidence-gated approach reaches 85 percent agreement at 63.2 percent coverage with a 91.0 percent guarantee success rate. Their confidence signal cut expected calibration error roughly in half compared to using raw token probability.

**Self-consistency disagreement as an abstention signal is a real but incomplete signal, and the literature disagrees on how far to trust it.** "Rating Roulette" (arxiv.org/abs/2510.27106, October 31, 2025, cited above) shows disagreement across repeated judge runs is common and that majority voting over disagreement improves accuracy. But a more targeted 2026 audit found that on GPQA, GPT-4.1 answers with self-consistency of 0.8 or higher are still wrong 48 percent of the time (95 percent CI 0.40 to 0.56), and that the model with the highest self-consistency in their test had the worst selective-prediction curve of the models tested, directly undercutting "abstain only when the judge disagrees with itself" as a sufficient rule on its own. Source: "When LLMs Agree, Are They Right? Auditing Self-Consistency and Cross-Model Agreement as Confidence Signals," Ding, arxiv.org/abs/2607.08065, July 28, 2026. Their adaptive stopping rule (stop sampling once consistency stabilizes) does cut sampling cost by about 60 percent for equivalent majority-vote error, which is useful for cost control even if the confidence signal itself is not fully trustworthy.

**Verbalized confidence calibration.** Asking a model to state confidence in words, rather than reading off token probability, reduces expected calibration error substantially but unevenly by benchmark: for ChatGPT, ECE fell from 0.140 to 0.054 on TriviaQA (minus 61 percent) and 0.256 to 0.065 on SciQ (minus 75 percent) but only 0.451 to 0.203 on TruthfulQA (minus 55 percent); for GPT-4, comparable reductions ranged from 47 to 74 percent depending on benchmark. "Often reducing ECE by a relative 50 percent" is a fair headline but the true range is roughly 19 to 75 percent depending on task. Source: "Just Ask for Calibration," Tian et al., arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975, EMNLP 2023. The foundational papers "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know" (Kadavath et al., Anthropic, arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221, 2022) and "Teaching Models to Express Their Uncertainty in Words" (Lin, Hilton, Evans, arxiv.org/abs/2205.14334, TMLR 2022) establish that larger models are reasonably well calibrated when asked to self-evaluate, but we could not pull exact ECE numbers from either primary source in this pass, a coverage gap.

**Conformal prediction for LLM judges is an active 2025 to 2026 sub-literature with real, quantified guarantees.** "SCOPE: Selective Conformal Optimized Pairwise LLM Judging," arxiv.org/abs/2602.13110, February 2026, ICML 2026, calibrates an acceptance threshold so error among non-abstained pairwise verdicts stays at or below a target risk, using bidirectional preference entropy (querying both response orders) as a bias-neutral uncertainty signal. At a target risk of 0.10, empirical risk stayed at 0.097 to 0.099 (the guarantee held) while coverage reached 0.89 to 0.98 depending on benchmark and judge scale, and the method accepted up to 2.4 times more judgments than naive abstention baselines under the same risk constraint. "Diagnosing LLM Judge Reliability: Conformal Prediction Sets and Transitivity Violations," Gupta and Kumar, arxiv.org/abs/2604.15302, April 16, 2026, applies split conformal prediction to Likert-scale judge scores and finds conformal set width correlates with reliability (Spearman 0.576, n equals 1,918, p less than 10 to the minus 100), so a wide prediction set is itself a usable abstention trigger. Two further papers on this topic exist but we could not extract their numeric results (PDF extraction failures): "Analyzing Uncertainty of LLM-as-a-Judge: Interval Evaluations with Conformal Prediction," arxiv.org/abs/2509.18658, September 23, 2025; and "ConU: Conformal Uncertainty in Large Language Models with Correctness Coverage Guarantees," arxiv.org/abs/2407.00499.

**Net recommendation for our harness:** combine at least two independent abstention signals (self-consistency disagreement plus a verbalized or conformal confidence measure) rather than relying on either alone, since self-consistency alone has documented failure cases at the exact confidence level (0.8) a naive threshold would use.

## 4. Validating a judge against human labels

**Sample size guidance is inconsistent across framings, and this should be stated plainly rather than papered over.** Practical eval-industry guidance clusters around small numbers: Braintrust recommends 50 to 100 labeled examples per scoring dimension, focused on rubric boundary cases (braintrust.dev, "LLM-as-a-judge vs human-in-the-loop evals"). Arize recommends 30 to 50 examples for early rubric iteration, treating results under about 100 examples as directional only, and notes that a 200-example test set with a 5 percent failure rate contains only about ten failure cases on average, too few to trust for per-class accuracy (arize.com/blog, "How to measure human-LLM judge alignment"). Anthropic's own guidance recommends starting with 20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures, on the logic that early-stage changes have large effect sizes so small samples suffice (see section 7). Against this, the formal statistics of pinning down Cohen's kappa to a given confidence interval width require far larger samples: illustrative values from the kappaSize methodology (Flack et al. 1988) show that constraining the 95 percent CI on kappa to within 0.20 needs roughly 118 subjects, within 0.05 needs roughly 1,625, and within 0.01 needs roughly 38,203 (serdarbalci.com/meddecide, summarizing the R kappaSize package). A companion source states more simply that with 500 items the 95 percent CI on a moderate kappa is roughly plus or minus 0.03, and 1,000-plus items are needed to pin kappa to two decimal places (zeroentropy.dev, "Cohen's kappa: inter-annotator agreement beyond raw percent"). These two framings, small samples for deployment decisions versus much larger samples for precise kappa estimation, are answering different questions and should not be conflated: 50 to 100 examples is a reasonable bar for "is this judge good enough to ship," not for "we know kappa to two decimal places."

**Kappa targets in practice also vary by source, and some vendors explicitly reject a single fixed threshold.** The canonical Landis and Koch 1977 bands (0.61 to 0.80 "substantial," 0.81 to 1.00 "almost perfect") get reused with deployment-specific relabeling: one industry source ties kappa 0.41 to 0.60 to "usable with human review on flagged cases," 0.61 to 0.79 to "acceptable for low-stakes automation," and 0.80 or above to "deploy autonomously in CI" (AWS sample repo, "cohens-kappa-for-llm-judges.md"). Another source targets 0.7 to 0.8 as matching the human-human agreement ceiling on subjective tasks (zeroentropy.dev). Braintrust and Arize both explicitly argue against any single universal threshold, recommending the bar be set per task risk and that raw agreement, kappa, and a full confusion matrix be reported together rather than a single number gating a decision. Both stances appear in current practitioner guidance; we present both rather than picking one.

**Which verdicts to have humans audit.** Every source that addressed this recommends stratified or active sampling over random sampling: strata suggested include judge confidence (low, mid, high, per class), disagreement across repeated judge samples, output category, and recency of the prompt version (Galileo AI blog, "How to Calibrate Your LLM Judge With Human Annotations"). An ECIR 2026 paper formalizes this for search relevance judgments and reports that stratifying by features of the LLM's own output (assigned label, probability, perplexity) reduces the human annotation budget needed to validate agreement while preserving a statistical guarantee, with the LLM's own label found to be the most useful stratification feature; we could not verify the specific magnitude of the reduction (paywalled beyond the abstract). Source: Merlo, Marchesin, Faggioli, Ferro, ECIR 2026, DOI 10.1007/978-3-032-21289-4_27. A frequently repeated but unverified figure ("stratified sampling reduces required human validation by up to 85 percent compared to random sampling") could not be traced to a primary source in this pass and should be treated as unconfirmed if used.

**Re-validation cadence when the judge model changes.** Practitioner guidance again converges on a heuristic rather than a derived rule: one source recommends recalibrating quarterly, or after any 60 to 90 day window, since kappa drifts as the underlying agent changes (AWS sample repo); another recommends recalibrating immediately after any judge model swap or vendor version bump, citing a real example where a model update shifted scores unevenly across prompts, one prompt dropping 9.6 percent in accuracy while another gained 5.1 percent from the same underlying model update, meaning a single spot check after an upgrade is not sufficient (Galileo AI blog). We found no controlled study deriving an optimal re-validation cadence; treat the quarterly or per-upgrade cadence as heuristic, not evidence-based, and budget for re-validating every judged dimension separately after a model swap rather than one representative prompt.

## 5. Semantic equivalence judging of short text values

This is directly relevant to judge (a). The best available benchmark comparing LLM judges to fine-tuned classifiers on paraphrase detection found LLMs are not more accurate than fine-tuned classifiers, only more flexible: the best LLM setup (Llama3, one prompt variant) reached 20.9 percent error overall versus 27.5 percent for a fine-tuned XLM-R model, but on the narrow classification objective alone the fine-tuned classifier actually won (34.2 percent error versus 41.5 percent for the LLM). The paper documents concrete structural failures: passive-voice rewording alone was enough to confuse every tested prompt variant, along with particle-placement changes and outright contradictions misclassified as paraphrases. Source: "PARAPHRASUS: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Evaluating Paraphrase Detection Models," arxiv.org/html/2409.12060v2, 2024. Entity matching research similarly finds LLMs competitive with, but not clearly superior to, fine-tuned matchers, with the advantage being few-shot robustness to unseen entities rather than raw accuracy; we could not extract exact per-dataset F1 numbers from the primary source in this pass. Source: "Entity Matching using Large Language Models," Peeters and Bizer, arxiv.org/abs/2310.11244, October 17, 2023, EDBT 2025.

**Where LLMs specifically fail: negation is the best-documented failure category.** On negation-focused natural language inference benchmarks, accuracy is well below non-negated baselines across model scales: GPT-J-6B scores 21.1 to 49.0 percent depending on the negation benchmark, GPT-3 175B scores 26.7 to 52.5 percent, and even InstructGPT, the best of the three tested, only reaches 54.8 to 76.7 percent, and the paper documents an inverse scaling trend where smaller models occasionally match or beat larger ones on some negation subsets. Source: "Language models are not naysayers: An analysis of language models on negation benchmarks," arxiv.org/html/2306.08189, 2023. Numbers and dates are the other classic failure categories in the broader NLI stress-test literature (numerical substitution, antonymy, over-reliance on word overlap), documented as categories in "Stress Test Evaluation for Natural Language Inference," Naik et al., aclanthology.org/C18-1198.pdf, ACL/COLING 2018, though we could not extract the exact per-category accuracy drops from this PDF in this pass. We found no dedicated benchmark on unit confusion specifically (for example, five pounds versus five kilograms wrongly judged equivalent); the clearest illustration we found is anecdotal rather than benchmarked: an agent issued a refund of 4,200 dollars instead of 42 dollars due to a decimal or unit confusion, and an LLM judge scored the response 0.89 out of 1 for helpfulness, entirely missing the numeric error, while a deterministic parser would have caught it in microseconds. Source: Rishav Hada, Future AGI, "How I Built Deterministic LLM Eval Metrics," futureagi.com/blog, March 13, 2026, updated May 20, 2026.

**The case for a cached equivalence table.** The same source reports concrete production economics that support memoizing judgments rather than re-invoking an LLM per comparison: an LLM-judge-only pipeline cost about 40,000 dollars in its first month at roughly one million traces per day and five judge calls per trace; adding deterministic pre-checks (schema validation, regex, canonical-form comparison) ahead of the judge caught about 60 percent of the failures the judge would have caught, at 0.003 dollars each, and a full cascade of deterministic checks before invoking the judge saved about 90 percent of judge cost on one metric with no measurable drop in detection rate. The same source states that seven of nine of their evaluation subtypes, including integer matching, currency-normalized scalars, yes-or-no decisions, and date canonicalization, admit deterministic normalization rather than needing a fresh LLM judgment each time. This is exactly the shape of argument for a cached equivalence table for judge (a): normalize and canonicalize where possible (numbers, currencies, dates, units), cache the LLM's verdict on any pair of raw strings once made, and only call the judge fresh for genuinely novel pairs, both for cost and because the failure modes above (negation, numbers, units) are precisely where a canonicalization step catches errors the LLM would miss.

## 6. Policy rule checking against transcripts

**tau2-bench's nl_assertions.** Sierra AI's tau2-bench splits task evaluation into environment assertions (database state), communication assertions (information conveyed), natural language assertions (free-text checks such as "the agent diagnosed the cause of the issue," used for easier debugging), and action assertions (required agent actions); in the telecom domain, only assertion functions are used at all. Source: "tau2-Bench: Evaluating Conversational Agents in a Dual-Control Environment," Barres, Dong, Ray, Si, Narasimhan, arxiv.org/html/2506.07982v1. We could not verify an 18.5 percent misalignment figure specifically tied to tau2 nl_assertions anywhere, including the paper itself, its GitHub repository, or related audit papers; the 18.5 percent figure that does exist in the literature is the unrelated cross-benchmark aggregate described in section 1, and we recommend not citing 18.5 percent as an nl_assertions-specific number. What we did verify is a broader validity audit of tau-bench's evaluation methodology finding that a trivial agent returning empty responses is judged successful on intentionally impossible tasks 38 percent of the time, and that the benchmark allows agents to exploit answer-listing to overestimate performance by 40 percent, plus an explicit note that the benchmark does not specify how negation modifiers should be handled during evaluation. Source: "Establishing Best Practices for Building Rigorous Agentic Benchmarks," arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5, 2025. Sierra has since released tau2-bench-verified and a successor, tau3-bench, specifically to fix annotation and task-definition errors: a February 2026 changelog reports 27 of 50 (54 percent) airline tasks and 26 of 114 (22.8 percent) retail tasks required fixes, 32.3 percent combined, though these are annotation-error rates, not judge-misalignment rates per se. Source: taubench.com/blog/tau3-task-fixes.html, February 2026; underlying analysis in "SABER: Small Actions, Big Errors," arxiv.org/abs/2512.07850, November 26, 2025.

**AgentDojo takes a different, deterministic approach worth noting by contrast.** It does not use an LLM judge for task or policy compliance at all: each task has a hand-written utility function that inspects model output and environment-state mutations directly, plus a ground-truth tool-call sequence for consistency checks. Source: "AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents," arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352, NeurIPS 2024. This is a useful data point: one credible agent-evaluation benchmark deliberately avoids LLM judges for compliance checking in favor of code, which lines up with our harness's own design of a deterministic verifier first, LLM judge only where code cannot express the rule.

**Meta's ARE (Agents Research Environments) and Gaia2.** ARE hosts environments with their own verifiers and the Gaia2 benchmark, which specifically stresses ambiguity, dynamic environments, and temporal constraints. We could not find a published quantitative reliability or misalignment number for its verifiers; its verifier design is described qualitatively only. Source: arxiv.org/abs/2509.17158, September 21, 2025, revised December 10, 2025.

**Deterministic gates recovering silent policy violations.** A 2026 paper documents that LLM judges evaluating tool-using agents on tau-bench can silently miss policy violations entirely, creating false confidence, and proposes replacing or augmenting LLM judgment with deterministic verification of tool calls and their parameters wherever the policy can be expressed that way. We could not extract the paper's precise miss-rate from the source (PDF extraction failed), but the qualitative claim, that LLM judges silently pass policy violations a deterministic gate would catch, is directly relevant to our design and matches the "40 percent constraint misrepresentation" theme from section 1. Source: "Reason Less, Verify More: Deterministic Gates Recover a Silent Policy-Violation Failure Mode in Tool-Using LLM Agents," arxiv.org/abs/2607.07405, 2026.

**The best hard number for how often an LLM judge misjudges agent behavior against a rule or outcome remains AgentRewardBench** (section 1): no judge exceeds 70 percent precision, meaning roughly 30 percent or more of judged-successful trajectories are false positives, with grounding mismatch and accepting an agent's false self-reported success named as the two leading failure modes. This is the single most load-bearing number for justifying our design of judges (b) and (d): a bare LLM judge reading a transcript and reasoning "in prose" about policy compliance should be expected to have a real, non-trivial false-positive rate on plausible-sounding but wrong verdicts, motivating structured output, abstention, and (for the dispute path) tool access to the actual database rather than the transcript alone.

## 7. What Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google publish

**Anthropic.** "Demystifying evals for AI agents," anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents, January 9, 2026. Key points: define a strict vocabulary (task or trial, transcript or trace including tool calls, outcome as final environment state, grader, suite); run multiple trials per task to account for stochastic variance; grade what the agent produced, not the path it took, so valid alternative solutions are not penalized; build partial credit into multi-component tasks rather than binary pass or fail; combine code-based, model-based, and human grading rather than relying on one channel; grade each dimension with an isolated LLM judge rather than one judge covering every dimension at once; make graders resistant to bypasses, so that passing genuinely requires solving the problem; give judges an explicit "unknown" option rather than forcing a guess, and calibrate them closely against human experts before trusting them; manually read transcripts across many trials to distinguish real agent failures from grader defects; and start with a small set, 20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures, since early-stage changes have large effect sizes.

**OpenAI.** There is no single canonical "evals cookbook" page under that exact name; guidance is spread across the (sunsetting) Evals platform docs, a cookbook notebook, and a graders reference page. Sources: developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evals and /graders and /evaluation-best-practices (undated, noting the Evals platform itself goes read-only October 31, 2026 and shuts down November 30, 2026); github.com/openai/openai-cookbook, "Custom-LLM-as-a-Judge.ipynb." Key points: a rubric's job is narrow, to highlight the crux of what is being tested, not to be exhaustive; model graders take chat-style templated input (reference answer plus sample output) and return structured output with a numeric result plus reasoning steps; classification into a small number of labeled categories beat a 1 to 10 numeric scale in their own test (98.15 percent versus 95.35 percent accuracy against known cases); interestingly, adding chain-of-thought reasoning before the verdict slightly hurt accuracy in that same test (92.10 percent versus 95.35 percent), a real tension with the more common recommendation to use chain-of-thought, worth weighing rather than resolving; use forced structured output (JSON schema, an enum for the verdict) at temperature 0 for reproducibility; and validate any model grader's agreement with human labels (a meta-eval) before trusting it in production. Because the Evals platform is being sunset, treat this guidance as somewhat in flux rather than a stable long-term reference.

**Google Vertex AI.** cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs (several pages did not fully render via automated fetch and are cited secondhand through a technical blog and search results, a coverage gap noted below). Key points: two evaluation modes, pointwise (score one candidate against explicit criteria) and pairwise (compare a candidate against a baseline to pick a winner); criteria and a separate rating rubric are both explicit, named structures (for example, a plain-language criterion plus a rubric mapping discrete score levels, such as 3 meaning strong on both criteria, to descriptions); the same prompt-template mechanism (PointwiseMetricPromptTemplate, PairwiseMetricPromptTemplate) is reused across different judged dimensions by swapping input variables; the default judge model is typically Gemini, with specialized judge models used for domain-specific cases such as translation quality; and Google's materials cite general research suggesting roughly 80 percent agreement between LLM judges and human evaluators as justification for the pointwise and pairwise autorater approach broadly, a general citation rather than a Google-specific benchmark. Source: atamel.dev/posts/2025/07-08_genai_eval_service_model_metrics, a Google Developer Expert writeup, July 8, 2025, used to substitute for Vertex documentation pages that would not render in automated fetch.

## 8. What this means for our four judges

Two numbers should anchor every design decision below. First, the best LLM judges evaluating agent trajectories reach at most 69 to 70 percent precision on the best current agent-trajectory benchmark (AgentRewardBench), meaning a bare LLM judge reading a transcript should be assumed wrong on the order of 20 to 30 percent of the time on plausible-looking cases, not the 5 percent error rate that intuition from chat-response grading (MT-Bench's roughly 80 to 85 percent agreement) might suggest. Second, self-consistency disagreement is a real but incomplete abstention signal (GPT-4.1 answers at 0.8 self-consistency were still wrong 48 percent of the time in one 2026 audit), so no single confidence signal should gate abstention alone.

**(a) Semantic equivalence of two free-text field values, with a cached equivalence table.**
Prompt structure: pairwise, reference-guided. Show the judge exactly two raw strings (the recorded reference value and the candidate value) plus the field's semantic type if known (free-text category, currency amount, date, quantity-plus-unit). Do not show surrounding transcript context; the paraphrase-detection literature's documented failures (passive voice, particle placement) are lexical, not context-dependent, so extra context adds noise without adding signal. Before calling the judge at all, run deterministic canonicalization: normalize numbers, currencies, dates, and units, and check the cache of previously judged (value_a, value_b) pairs (order-independent key). Only call the LLM on cache misses. Output schema: `{ verdict: "equivalent" | "not_equivalent" | "abstain", confidence: float, cited_spans: [substrings from each value that justify the verdict], normalization_applied: bool }`. Require the judge to quote the specific substrings driving its decision (even though we could not find a benchmarked ablation proving this reduces errors, it costs little and forces the model to ground its answer rather than pattern-match on overall fluency, consistent with the tool-verification result that grounding in something checkable, there code execution, raised accuracy from under 42 to about 72 percent). Samples: 3, majority vote on the equivalent or not_equivalent label; abstain if the majority is not achieved (2 of 3 does not agree) or if either sample flags a number, unit, date, or negation mismatch, since that is exactly where LLMs are documented to fail silently. Temperature: 0 is not compatible with the 3-sample majority-vote design (temperature 0 removes the diversity that makes majority voting meaningful), so sample at a low but non-zero temperature (for example 0.3) as the "Necessity of Setting Temperature" paper's own data suggests error rate is still near zero in that range while consistency remains high. Validation set: given this is a narrow binary task with a cache that will accumulate coverage over time, 50 to 100 diverse human-labeled pairs is consistent with the Braintrust or Arize guidance, oversampling pairs involving numbers, negation, dates, and units specifically since that is where the documented failure modes concentrate; re-validate any time the equivalence-cache schema or normalization rules change, and re-run the full validation set (not a spot check) after any judge model swap, given the documented case of a model update helping one prompt and hurting another by comparable margins. Audit rate line: since this judge feeds a cache, the audit dashboard should show agreement stratified separately for cache-hit pairs (should be at or near 100 percent, since a human already reviewed the disagreements that seeded the cache) versus fresh LLM judgments, and within fresh judgments, agreement broken out by whether the pair involved a number, unit, date, or negation, since aggregate agreement will hide a much worse number in exactly that slice.

**(b) Checking a natural-language policy rule against a transcript.**
Prompt structure: pointwise, checklist-decomposed rather than holistic. Do not ask "did the agent follow policy," ask a small set of atomic yes-or-no sub-questions derived from the rule (for example, for "confirm before cancelling": did the agent state the specific action it was about to take before performing it, and did the agent wait for or receive an affirmative response before performing it). The checklist-decomposition literature's 0.45 average agreement improvement and the CheckEval methodology are the strongest evidence we found for this design choice. Input to show: the full transcript up to and including the End state, redacted of any system or developer text unrelated to the policy being checked, since the literature has no evidence for hiding context helping beyond practitioner folk wisdom, but it costs nothing here and matches Anthropic's own recommendation to grade each dimension in isolation. Input to hide: other unrelated policy rules being checked in the same run, so a decomposition-per-rule design should call the judge once per policy rule rather than once per transcript. Output schema: `{ sub_answers: [{question, answer: yes/no/abstain, cited_span}], verdict: pass/fail/abstain, confidence: float }`, with the overall verdict computed deterministically from the sub-answers (a fixed logical combination) rather than asked of the model directly, so the LLM's job is narrowed to the atomic questions where it is measurably more reliable. Samples: 3 per atomic sub-question, majority vote per sub-question, abstain the whole verdict if any sub-question fails to reach majority. Validation set: this is the judge most exposed to the AgentRewardBench-class failure (30-plus percent false-positive rate on trajectory success), so we recommend a larger validation set here than for (a): 100 to 150 human-labeled transcripts per distinct policy rule, stratified so the false-success and sycophancy-to-confident-language failure modes documented in section 1 (agents whose final message asserts success get scored more "complete" regardless of truth) are deliberately oversampled; audit dashboard should report agreement separately for transcripts whose final agent message is confidently phrased versus hedged, since that is the documented axis where sycophancy bites. Re-validate on every policy-rule wording change and every judge model swap.

**(c) Confirming a recorded reference run is actually a good reference, with abstention to a human.**
Prompt structure: this is closest to the "Trust or Escalate" design (arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370) and should borrow it directly: sample the judge under a few different framings or personas, use cross-sample agreement as the primary confidence signal, and escalate to a human whenever confidence falls under a calibrated threshold, rather than trying to hit high accuracy at full coverage. Input to show: the full candidate reference transcript, the task specification it was meant to satisfy, and the deterministic verifier's own output on that run's End state (if the deterministic verifier already flags a problem, the judge should not be asked to independently re-decide that; it should only be asked about the parts code cannot check, matching Anthropic's own "grade what code cannot" principle). Input to hide: any information about how many times this reference has already been accepted or how it compares to other candidate references for the same task, to avoid anchoring. Output schema: `{ verdict: good_reference / bad_reference / abstain, confidence: float, cited_spans: [transcript excerpts showing why], failure_category: enum or null }`. Samples and thresholding: follow the cascaded design directly, since it is the only source in this review with a measured, guaranteed coverage-accuracy tradeoff for exactly this kind of judge decision (85 percent agreement at 63.2 percent coverage in their reported setting); given that a bad reference silently poisons every future comparison against it, bias the threshold toward abstaining more, not less, than their reported operating point. Validation set: because errors here compound (a bad reference corrupts all downstream deterministic comparisons), this is the judge that most needs a real kappa estimate, not just a directional check; budget for 150 to 300 human-labeled candidate references sampled stratified by task type, oversampling anything the automated confidence signal scored as borderline, consistent with every audit-sampling source reviewed in section 4. Audit rate line: report agreement separately for the abstained-and-escalated population (should approach 100 percent since a human ultimately decided) versus the auto-accepted population (this is the number that matters, and should be tracked against the guarantee, not just reported as an aggregate).

**(d) Dispute path: judging an End state outside the known required or allowed set, with tools to inspect the database.**
Prompt structure: agentic judge with tool access, not a plain transcript reader. Agent-as-a-Judge is the strongest evidence for this design (raising alignment with human consensus by roughly 4 to 30 percentage points and cutting cost by 97.6 percent versus a plain LLM judge on the same systems), and the tool-verification result (raising agreement from under 42 to about 72 percent) reinforces it. Give the judge read access to the database (query tools) so it can check the actual current state directly rather than trust the transcript's account of it, matching AgentDojo's and Anthropic's shared principle of grading the actual outcome, not the narrated path. Input to show: the transcript, the End state under dispute, the known required and allowed sets it fell outside of, and query tools scoped to read-only access on the relevant tables; the judge should be required to run at least one verifying query before returning a verdict, not merely reason about what the database probably contains. Input to hide: nothing needs to be hidden here beyond normal least-privilege scoping of the tools themselves (read-only, scoped to the tables relevant to this task), since this judge exists precisely because the situation is novel and under-specified. Output schema: `{ verdict: acceptable / unacceptable / abstain, confidence: float, queries_run: [...], cited_evidence: [query results and/or transcript spans], reasoning_summary: string }`. Samples: given the cost of tool calls, 1 primary run with a required self-check step (the judge is asked to state what evidence would change its mind, then check whether that evidence is present) rather than 3 independent samples; if budget allows, run a second sample with a different query strategy and abstain on disagreement, since this is the judge with the least existing benchmark coverage (novel, out-of-set End states have no direct benchmark analogue in the literature we found) and therefore the one where we have the least basis for trusting a single pass. Validation set: because disputes are by definition the tail of the distribution and will accumulate slowly, do not wait for a large set before shipping; start with every historical dispute case (even a few dozen) fully human-labeled, and treat every future dispute as an opportunity to grow this set, auditing 100 percent of dispute verdicts by a human until the set reaches at least 50, then move to stratified sampling as in section 4. Audit rate line: report agreement against human review for every dispute verdict until the validation set is large enough for the aggregate line to be statistically meaningful (given the section 4 numbers, that is realistically in the 50 to 100 case range for a directional read, well short of the 1,000-plus needed to pin down kappa precisely), and always report the abstention rate alongside agreement, since a judge that abstains on everything looks perfectly "safe" on a naive agreement metric.

**Where the evidence is thin, restated plainly:**
- Evidence-citation (quote the exact span) as a standalone accuracy-improving technique for judges is not benchmarked anywhere we found; we recommend it in every schema above on design-logic grounds (grounding, auditability), not because a paper measured its effect.
- Hiding irrelevant context from the judge is practitioner folk wisdom, not a benchmarked ablation.
- Unit-confusion-specific failure rates for LLM judges (as opposed to the negation and general-numeracy literature) are not benchmarked anywhere we found; the one illustration we have is a single anecdote from an industry blog, not a study.
- The oft-repeated figures of "18.5 percent nl_assertions misalignment" and "40 percent constraint misrepresentation from AgenticAI-Supervisor" both trace to real papers, but not in the specific framing they are often repeated in: the 18.5 percent is a four-benchmark aggregate validity-audit rate, and the 40 percent is a single paper's own RL-training-environment framework name and its own reward-signal reliability statistic, not an established external benchmark. Both are usable as directional evidence, not as precise, reproducible judge-accuracy statistics for our specific judges.
- Position-swap-and-average, the classic 2023 fix for position bias, appears to no longer reliably help on modern frontier judges and sometimes hurts, per a 2026 replication; anyone implementing swap-and-average based on the older MT-Bench or FairEval recommendation should be aware the newer evidence is mixed.
